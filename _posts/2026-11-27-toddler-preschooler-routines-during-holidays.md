Hello readers, it has been a while! And honestly, we could not think of a better time to come back than the holiday season.

December is busy for everyone. There are family dinners, school holiday programs, travel, late nights and lots of sugar. For adults it is incredibly fun. But for a 2, 3 or 4 year old, it can feel like the whole world has suddenly changed. And when their world changes, the meltdowns follow.

The good news is that you do not need to hold on to your full daily schedule to keep things calm. **The simplest way to keep a toddler or preschooler's routine during the holidays is to protect a few anchors - sleep, meals and some quiet time - and let the rest be flexible.** Add a little warning before every big change, and most of the holiday season becomes much easier for the whole family.

Let us walk you through how Tanya and Tanmay managed this when Ryan was 2 and Sophie was 6. We are sure many of you will find your own family somewhere in their story.

## Why do toddlers and preschoolers melt down during the holidays?

Young children feel safe when they know what comes next. Their routine tells them that breakfast comes after brushing their teeth, play comes after breakfast and a nap comes after lunch. During the holidays, this order disappears almost overnight.

Add to it new places, loud rooms, lots of people and bedtime getting later every day. Their little bodies get tired and their brains get overloaded. They do not have the skills to explain, "I am overwhelmed", so they show it instead - crying, hitting, clinging or refusing everything.

Tanya noticed this the very first week of winter break. Ryan, who was usually happy at breakfast, started crying over the "wrong" cup. Two days later he threw himself on the floor at the mall because he could not press the elevator button. It was not about the cup or the elevator button. It was about everything else that had changed that week.

## Which parts of the routine matter most during the holidays?

You cannot control every party invite or flight time. So, rather focus on these three anchors:

**1. Sleep:** Keep wake-up time and bedtime within about an hour of the usual, as much as possible. If one night runs late, bring the next one back on track. For most 2 and 3 year olds, skipping naps for many days in a row is a big NO NO. Even a nap in the stroller or car counts.

**2. Meals and snacks:** Holiday food comes at odd times. A hungry toddler at a 7 pm dinner party is a meltdown waiting to happen. Tanya always carried a small snack box with crackers, cheese and fruit for Sophie and fed Ryan before they left home.

**3. Quiet time:** Children need a break from noise and people. It can be 15 minutes with a book, a puzzle in a quiet room, or simply cuddling on the couch. This is the part that most families forget, and it is often the one that makes the biggest difference.

Everything else - the outings, the shopping, the visits - can move around these three.

## How do I keep bedtime consistent while travelling?

That December, the family flew to spend a week at Ryan's grandparents. Tanya knew the new house, new bed and time difference would be hard on him. So she packed his bedtime routine along with his clothes.

Ryan's soft blanket, his two favourite storybooks, his white noise and his night light went in the carry-on bag. At grandma's house, they followed the same steps as home every night: bath, pyjamas, brushing teeth, two stories and lights off. The room was different but the steps were the same. By the third night, Ryan was falling asleep almost as easily as he did at home.

A few more things that help:

- Keep the same order of bedtime steps, even if the timing shifts a little.
- Bring something from home that smells and feels familiar. As simple as their own pillowcase, a favourite soft toy or the same diffuser scent you use in their room at home.
- Use blackout curtains or a dark towel over the window if the room is too bright for mid-day naps.
- Expect the first two nights to be harder and do not panic.

## How can I prepare my child for a big family gathering?

Surprises are fun for adults. For young children, they can be extremely stressful. So, make sure to talk about the event before it happens.

A day before the family dinner, Tanmay showed both Sophie and Ryan photos of their cousins and aunts who would be there. He told them what would happen: "We will drive to Aunty's house. There will be lots of people and yummy food. You can play with Zara and her trains. After dinner, we will come back home and sleep."

Sophie, who was 6, had a different worry. She did not like being hugged and kissed by relatives she barely remembered. Tanya and Sophie practised other ways to say hello - a high five, a wave or a fist bump. Sophie felt in control, and no one was upset.

Giving your child a small job also helps a lot. Ryan's job was to hand out the napkins at dinner. It kept him busy and made him feel important instead of lost in the crowd.

## How do I handle transitions like leaving a party or the park?

Transitions are the hardest part of the day for little ones. Stopping something fun to do something else is a big ask for a 5 year old.

At the holiday light festival, Sophie did not want to leave the train display. Tanmay tried what every parent tries first - "Come on, we are leaving now!" It did not go well.

The next year, they did it differently:

- **Give a warning:** "Five more minutes with the trains, then we go to the car."
- **Make it visible:** Set a timer on the phone and let your child watch it or press stop.
- **Count it down:** "Two more rounds of the train, then bye-bye trains."
- **Give them something to look forward to:** "In the car, you can choose the song."

It took a little more patience, but the walk back to the car was calm. Sophie even waved bye to the train.

For younger toddlers, a simple picture schedule works wonders. Draw three boxes on paper - car, Grandma's house, home - and let your child tick each one off. It helps them see what is coming next.

## What should I do when a meltdown happens anyway?

Even with all the planning, meltdowns may still happen. And that is okay. Your child is not being naughty. They are telling you they have had too much.

When Ryan fell apart at the end of a long dinner, Tanya picked him up and took him to a quiet bedroom. She did not try to reason with him or ask him to stop crying. She sat with him, spoke softly and waited. Once he calmed down, she named it for him: "That was a lot of people and a lot of noise. Your body got tired." Only then did they decide it was time to go home.

Remember, your calm helps them find their calm. You can always talk about it later, when everyone is rested.

So readers, the holidays do not have to be perfect to be happy. A few steady anchors and a little preparation can make this season calmer for your little ones and for you too.
