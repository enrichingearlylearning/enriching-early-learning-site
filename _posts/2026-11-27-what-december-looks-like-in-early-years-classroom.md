It is a Tuesday morning in December. Three children are away travelling with their families. Two more arrived late and sleepy after a family party the night before. The holiday concert rehearsal is at 10 AM. And Liam has his hands over his ears before circle time has even started.

If you work in an early years classroom, this probably sounds very familiar.

Last year, [Aaliya](https://www.enrichingearlylearning.com/aaliya-life-as-an-educator/) rethought her holiday celebrations so that every child in her room felt they belonged. You can read about it in [Belonging in Early Years: How Holiday Joy Can Transform Classrooms](https://www.enrichingearlylearning.com/belonging-in-early-years-how-holiday-joy-can-transform-classrooms/). But as that December went on, she noticed something else. The celebrations were not the only problem. The whole month was simply too much.

## What does December look like in an early years classroom?

**December is often the most exciting and the most dysregulated month of the year in a preschool classroom.** Routines are interrupted by concerts, parties and special visitors. Attendance becomes irregular because of travel. Children come in tired from late nights at home. Many are excited about the break, and some are quietly worried about it. Educators are tired too. All of this shows up as more tears, more conflicts, more clinginess and shorter attention spans.

None of this means the children are being "naughty". It means their world is busier than their little nervous systems can manage.

## Why do young children's behaviours change in December?

Aaliya sat down with her assistant teacher, **Ms. Kiyara**, at the end of the second week of December. They wrote down everything that had felt hard that week. The list was long.

Sofia, who usually separated easily, cried at drop-off three mornings in a row. Liam was covering his ears and leaving the group much more often. Two children who were best friends were suddenly fighting over every toy. Priya had stopped eating her snack.

When they looked at the list together, the pattern was clear. Almost nothing about the classroom had stayed the same that week:

- Circle time had been shortened twice for concert practice.
- Outdoor time had been cancelled once for a holiday photo session.
- There had been a classroom party with extra sugar and a lot of noise.
- Many children were having late nights and busy weekends at home.
- Three children had already left for the holidays, and the group felt different.

Kiyara said it simply:

> "We keep adding special things. But we never take anything away."

## The holiday concert dilemma

The holiday concert was the biggest event of the month. Families loved it. The centre's director loved it. But during rehearsal, Aaliya watched Noah, one of her quietest children, freeze on the stage and start to cry. Liam refused to go near it at all.

Aaliya asked herself an honest question: *Who is this concert really for?*

She did not cancel it. Instead, she gave every child a choice of how to take part:

- Sing with the group
- Ring the jingle bells from the side
- Hold up the picture cards for the song
- Watch with Ms. Kiyara from the front row

Noah chose the bells. Liam chose to hold the picture cards, wearing his headphones. On the day, every child was part of the concert in their own way, and there were no tears on the stage. One parent told Aaliya afterwards that it was the first concert her son had enjoyed.

## Keeping the routine when the calendar is full

The next thing Aaliya and Kiyara did was protect the parts of the day that children depend on the most. They agreed on a few simple rules for the rest of the month:

**1. The anchors stay.** Arrival, circle time, snack, outdoor play and rest time would happen every day, at the usual time, even on special days.

**2. One special thing per day.** If there was a concert rehearsal on Monday, there would be no party or visitor that day.

**3. Show the change before it happens.** Aaliya added a bright "special event" card to the visual schedule. Each morning at circle time, she showed the children exactly where the special thing fit into their day.

**4. Calm time after every busy time.** After the party or the rehearsal, the class came back to a quiet activity - a story, playdough or soft music - before anything new.

Within a week, Kiyara noticed the difference. There were fewer conflicts after lunch and fewer tears at transitions. Sofia's drop-offs became easier too.

## Preparing children for the winter break

Many adults forget that a holiday break is also a goodbye. For some young children, two weeks away from their teachers, friends and routine can feel very big.

Aaliya helped her class get ready for the break:

- **A countdown calendar** showed "school days" and "home days" in two different colours, so children could see that school would come back.
- **A class photo book** with pictures of friends, teachers and the classroom was sent home with each child to look at during the break.
- **Talking about returning:** At circle time, the children talked about what they would do when they came back - who they wanted to play with and which toys they would choose first.

On the last day, Sofia hugged her photo book tightly and said, "I'll bring it back when it's school days again." That is exactly what Aaliya hoped she would feel.

## What parents can do to help

If you are a parent, let your child's educator know about any travel, visitors or late nights, and expect a little more tiredness and clinginess this month. It usually settles once routines return. Our post on [keeping toddler and preschooler routines during the holidays](https://www.enrichingearlylearning.com/toddler-preschooler-routines-during-holidays/) has simple ideas you can use at home.

## What you can do this week

If you are an educator, pick one of these to try this week:

- Look at your December calendar and remove one special event that is not really for the children.
- Add a "special event" card to your visual schedule.
- Give every child a choice of how to take part in the holiday concert.
- Start a countdown calendar that shows children when school comes back.
